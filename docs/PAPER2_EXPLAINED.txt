PAPER 2, EXPLAINED
"Stopping a Reach in VR: Measuring the Warning Time Required for
Vibrotactile Collision Avoidance"

For the meeting on 2026-09-21. Written to be read aloud.


================================================================================
1. THE ONE SENTENCE
================================================================================

We measure how much warning a person needs in order to stop their hand before
it hits something they cannot see.


================================================================================
2. WHY THIS IS WORTH DOING
================================================================================

Every VR headset warns you about real obstacles. Guardian, chaperone, and the
research behind them all ask the same kind of question: what should the warning
BE? A wall you can see? A sound? A buzz? On the wrist or the chest?

Nobody has asked WHEN it has to arrive.

That gap matters because a perfect warning delivered too late does nothing. If
your hand is 80 ms from a wall, no cue on earth will stop it. There is a
deadline, and the field has never measured it.

So the question is simple: what is the deadline?

Right now a designer picking a warning time has nothing to go on. We want to
hand them a number with an error bar.


================================================================================
3. WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS IN THE STUDY
================================================================================

One person, one visit, about 65 minutes, 360 trials.

They stand with the headset on. Their right hand rests at a marked home
position. Nothing is in the real room; it is cleared.

--- A single trial ---

  1. The display shows NORMAL or FAST. That tells them how quickly to reach.

  2. A ball appears about 70 cm in front of them.

  3. They reach out and touch it, then return to home.

  4. Somewhere along the path sits an invisible box, about 20 cm across. They
     cannot see it. They do not know where it is. It moves every trial.

--- Three out of four trials: nothing else happens ---

They reach straight through the invisible box and touch the ball. No buzz, no
sound, no score. They never learn they went through anything.

--- One out of four trials: their wrist buzzes ---

The rule they were taught is: when you feel the buzz, STOP and pull your hand
straight back. Do not finish the reach. Do not curve around anything.

The buzz is timed. On some trials it arrives about 400 ms before their hand
would have reached the box. On others, only about 60 ms before. Five different
timings, mixed randomly.

We record one thing: did the hand enter the box, or not?


================================================================================
4. THE PART THAT IS EASY TO MISS
================================================================================

--- We cannot deliver the buzz at an exact time, so we do not try ---

Three things get in the way. The tracking runs at 90 frames a second, so timing
is chunky. The haptic hardware adds its own delay. And we have to PREDICT when
the hand would have reached the box, which is never perfect.

So instead of aiming precisely, we aim roughly and then MEASURE what actually
got delivered. The x-axis of our result is what really happened on that trial,
not what we asked for.

That turns our biggest weakness into a non-issue. Scatter in the delivered
timing is fine. It actually fills in the curve more evenly than five exact
values would.

--- The no-warning trials are not filler ---

Those three-in-four trials with no buzz still have a hazard on the path. People
reach right through. That rate is the bottom of our curve: what happens with
ZERO warning.

Without it we would have no floor, and the curve would float.

--- Why only one in four gets a warning ---

If warnings were common, people would start slowing down and hovering, waiting
for one. Then every measurement would be of a cautious reach, not a normal one.
Twenty-five percent keeps them reaching naturally.


================================================================================
5. WHAT WE GET OUT
================================================================================

--- The main result: one curve ---

  Horizontal axis: how much warning the person actually received
  Vertical axis:   the chance they stopped in time

It is an S-shape. Near zero warning, almost nobody stops. With enough warning,
almost everybody does. Somewhere in between it rises.

From that curve we read off two numbers:

  LT50  the warning time where half of reaches get stopped
  LT80  the warning time where 80% get stopped   <-- this is the deliverable

We report LT80 with a confidence interval, and we recommend the UPPER end of
that interval, not the middle. If we are not sure, a safety system should get
the benefit of the doubt.

--- Second result: does speed change the answer? ---

That is what NORMAL and FAST are for. We expect a fast reach needs more warning,
because there is more momentum to kill. If so, a good system should warn earlier
when it sees you moving quickly.

--- Third result: do people differ? ---

We report how much the required warning time varies from person to person.

  If people differ a lot, one fixed setting cannot serve everyone, and
  personalised timing is worth researching.

  If they barely differ, one setting works for all, and nobody needs to build
  personalisation.

Either answer is useful. That is why it is worth reporting.

--- We also check our own assumption ---

We claim to know when the hand "would have" hit the box. On trials where the
person failed to stop, we know when they ACTUALLY hit it. So we compare our
prediction against the truth and report the error. That turns our largest
assumption into a measured number.


================================================================================
6. WHAT WE CAN AND CANNOT CLAIM
================================================================================

WE CAN SAY:
  "A VR safety system that warns later than X ms before contact will fail for
  most users, no matter how good the cue is."

  That is a floor. Floors are useful precisely because they do not depend on
  what the user is trying to do.

WE CANNOT SAY:
  "X is the best warning time." Earlier might be better, or might just be
  annoying. We do not measure annoyance.

  "This is what users will do in the real world." We TELL people to stop. A
  real user also has to notice the buzz, work out what it means, and decide to
  act. So our number is a LOWER BOUND on what a real system needs.

  Anything about trust or false alarms. Our warnings are always correct. You
  need an unreliable system to study trust, and that is a separate study.


================================================================================
7. QUESTIONS HE IS LIKELY TO ASK
================================================================================

Q: Why virtual obstacles instead of real ones?
   Three reasons. Safety: we need people to nearly hit things at speed.
   Control: the hazard has to move every trial so it cannot be memorised.
   And the thing we measure is a limit of the motor system, which does not
   care whether the obstacle is real.

Q: Why not just look up reaction time in the literature?
   Reaction time is how fast you can start something. This is how fast you can
   STOP something already moving, while still wanting to reach the target.
   Different problem, and nobody has measured it for a limb.

Q: How do you know when they would have hit it?
   We freeze the hand's speed and direction at the moment the buzz starts, and
   project forward. We never update it with what they did afterwards, because
   someone who successfully stops would otherwise look like they were warned
   infinitely early. And we check the prediction against real hits (section 5).

Q: Isn't the answer obvious? More warning is better.
   The direction is obvious. The number is not, and the number is what a
   designer actually needs. "Warn earlier" is not something you can build.
   "Warn at least 340 ms before contact" is.

Q: How many participants?
   24 to 30. The simulation says the interval is narrow enough at that size.
   The real driver is trials per person, not people, because the curve is
   fitted within each person.

Q: What if the interval comes out too wide to recommend anything?
   Then we say so. That is a publishable result: it would mean this quantity
   is more variable than the field assumes, which is itself worth knowing.
