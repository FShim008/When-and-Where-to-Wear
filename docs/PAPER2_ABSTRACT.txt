ABSTRACT  (188 words)
================================================================================

Virtual reality systems warn users about obstacles they cannot see, and a large
body of work has studied what those warnings should be: their modality, pattern,
placement, and visual form. How early a warning must arrive has not been measured.
This matters because a warning delivered too late cannot work, however well it is
designed. We report the first psychometric estimate of that deadline. [N]
participants performed [X] reaching trials in VR toward targets placed beyond
invisible hazard volumes. On one quarter of trials a vibrotactile cue on the
reaching arm instructed them to stop, delivered at one of five lead times spanning
[60-400] ms before predicted contact. Because delivery is imprecise by
construction, we fit the response curve against the lead time each trial actually
achieved rather than the one requested. Participants stopped [X]% of reaches with
no warning and [X]% with the longest. The fitted curve gives LT80, the warning
time at which four reaches in five are arrested, of [X] ms (95% CI [a, b]). Faster
reaches required [X] ms [more/less] warning, and the requirement varied across
participants by [X] ms. We recommend the interval's upper bound as a design floor.

================================================================================
NOTES ON THE WRITING
================================================================================

WHAT IT CLAIMS, AND WHAT IT DOES NOT
  "the first psychometric estimate of that deadline" is the strongest defensible
  claim, and it rests on the search record in PAPER2_RELATED_WORK.md Part V.
  Re-run that search before submission; if something turns up, soften to "a
  psychometric estimate".

  The abstract does NOT say this is the optimal warning time. It says LT80 and
  recommends a floor. Keep that distinction - it is the one a reviewer will test.

THE SENTENCE THAT CARRIES THE METHOD
  "we fit the response curve against the lead time each trial actually achieved
  rather than the one requested" - this pre-empts the obvious attack (you cannot
  deliver milliseconds precisely) by showing it was designed around, not missed.

THE ZERO-WARNING NUMBER IS LOAD-BEARING
  Reporting the no-warning stop rate in the abstract shows the curve has a
  measured floor. Without it a reader cannot tell whether the task produced
  genuine near-approaches. If that number comes out HIGH, the geometry failed and
  the paper needs rewriting, not just a different abstract sentence.

WHAT IS DELIBERATELY ABSENT
  - No mention of Paper 1. The papers stand alone (§2).
  - No claim about what users would do unprompted. Participants are instructed to
    stop, so this is a capacity bound. That belongs in the discussion, not here.
  - No "we show that more warning helps". True, uninteresting, and not the point.

IF THE INTERVAL COMES OUT TOO WIDE
  §16.2 allows "too wide to specify an operating point" as an honest result.
  Then the last two sentences become: "The interval on LT80 spans [a, b] ms, too
  wide to specify a single operating point. We report the bound and the sources of
  variance, and argue that warning timing requires per-application calibration."
  That is publishable. Do not narrow it by extrapolating past the sampled range.
