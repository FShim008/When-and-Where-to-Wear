WHY THE PREDICTIVE THRESHOLD IS T = 1.0 SECOND
Paper 1 · prepared 2026-09-16 · source: PAPER1_STUDY_DESIGN.md section 6


THE SHORT ANSWER

T = 1.0 s is not our guess at how much warning a person needs. It is the
smallest value that keeps the experiment valid.


THE PROBLEM

Our two conditions fire on different things.

  Reactive    fires when the hand is 0.30 m from the hazard.
  Predictive  fires 1.0 s before the predicted hit.

A fixed time is not a fixed distance. Predictive fires at a distance of
speed x T. So predictive is only earlier than reactive when speed x T is
greater than 0.30 m. The two rules swap order at:

  crossover speed = D / T

At T = 0.5 s that is 0.30 / 0.5 = 0.6 m/s. Anyone moving slower than 0.6 m/s
would get the "predictive" cue later than the reactive one. Normal arm speeds
fall on both sides of 0.6 m/s, so the manipulation would run backwards on some
trials and forwards on others.

That would make a null result impossible to read. "Predictive timing does not
help" and "the predictive cue was not actually earlier" would look the same in
the data.


WHAT WE TESTED

We ran the real trigger code against simulated reaches: minimum-jerk profiles
at 90 Hz, across movement times from 0.4 to 1.4 s. We measured how much clear
approach distance each setting needs before predictive reliably leads.

  T = 0.5 s   needs 0.55 m
  T = 0.8 s   needs 0.45 m
  T = 1.0 s   needs 0.40 m

Our task geometry guarantees 0.40 m. T = 1.0 s is the first value that fits.


WHY NOT LARGER

A larger T fires earlier and more often. That adds alerts the user does not
need. We treat that as a cost of the policy and measure it, so we want the
smallest value that works, not the largest.


IN ONE SENTENCE

T = 1.0 s is the smallest threshold that keeps reactive and predictive truly
different across the speeds and distances our task produces.


NOTE ON PAPER 2

This argument needs nothing from Paper 2. Paper 2 measures how much warning a
person needs. Paper 1 only needs its two conditions to differ in a consistent
direction, which is what the number above guarantees. We also log, on every
trial, when both rules would have fired, so we report the lead times we
actually delivered rather than assuming them.

Paper 2 cannot set T even if we wanted it to. Paper 2 measures lead times from
60 to 400 ms. T is 1000 ms, which is 2.5 times outside the range it samples.
What Paper 2 does give us is a check, after the fact: it tells us whether the
warning each policy delivered was long enough for a person to act on. That
belongs in the discussion, not in the choice of T.


AND WHERE DOES 0.30 M COME FROM?  (added 2026-09-23)

Same kind of answer. We are not claiming 0.30 m is the correct distance. We are
claiming it is a normal, usable setting for a proximity warning, and that the
comparison between the two policies is valid at that setting.

Three things back it up.

  1. We tried alternatives and wrote down why they lost. Dropping D to 0.25 m
     saves only 0.05 m of dead zone and makes the proximity cue less useful.
     The reactive condition has to stay a real warning, or the study becomes
     "useful versus useless" instead of a comparison of two warning policies.

  2. This is not our invented question. Car collision warning systems have been
     built both ways - fire at a fixed distance, or fire on predicted time to
     collision - since the 1990s, and there is a large literature on how warning
     timing changes what drivers do. We are moving a known question into VR.

  3. We can show the answer does not depend on the exact numbers. On every
     opportunity we record when BOTH rules would have fired. So we can rebuild
     what would have happened at other settings, from 0.20 to 0.45 m and 0.70 to
     1.50 s, and show the predictive rule still fires first almost everywhere.

The one fair criticism left is that we only tested one setting per policy. We
say that ourselves, in the limitations, before a reviewer can say it for us.


SOMEONE ALREADY RAN THIS COMPARISON  (added 2026-09-23)

We found a 2019 IEEE VR paper (Valkov and Linsen) that tested the same two
rules. They had people walk toward an invisible wall and warned them with
vibration in the headset padding. One version warned at a fixed distance. The
other scaled the distance by walking speed, which is the same rule as ours.

Two things follow, and we need both in the paper.

FIRST, IT GIVES US OUR NUMBERS. They used 600 ms to 1.6 s for the time-based
rule. Our 1.0 s sits inside that published range. So T is no longer only our
own simulation - a peer-reviewed paper at this venue used the same band.

SECOND, AND MORE IMPORTANT, THEY GOT THE OPPOSITE RESULT. The speed-based rule
caused MORE collisions, not fewer. We have to cite this and explain why we
expect something different, or a reviewer will do it for us.

Here is why we expect something different, and it is their own explanation.
Their vibration got STRONGER as you got closer and WEAKER as you slowed down.
So when people slowed, the warning faded. They learned they could creep forward
and keep the buzzing low. The warning became something to manage rather than
something to obey.

Our cue does not work that way. It fires once per approach and then stops. It
does not get stronger, weaker, or respond to speed at all. There is nothing to
manage. And the cue is exactly the same in both of our conditions - only the
moment it fires changes. In their study the timing and the cue design changed
together, so their result cannot separate the two.

That is actually a better reason to run our study than the one we had before.
We are not saying "nobody has tried this." We are saying "someone tried it, it
failed for a reason they identified themselves, and we fixed that reason."

NOTE: an earlier draft of our related work said no one had compared these two
rules. That was wrong. It is corrected now.
