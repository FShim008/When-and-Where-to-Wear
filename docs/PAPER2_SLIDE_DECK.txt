SLIDE 8 — THREE DECISIONS THAT MAKE IT WORK
================================================================================

ON SLIDE
  1.  We don't aim precisely — we measure what landed
      frames are chunky, hardware lags, prediction is imperfect

  2.  No-warning trials are the floor of the curve
      they still carry a hazard; people reach right through

  3.  25% warnings, never more
      common warnings make people hover and wait

SAY
  "One. We could fight for millisecond precision and lose. Instead we aim
  roughly and record what actually got delivered. The x-axis is what really
  happened. Scatter helps us, it fills the curve in.

  Two. Those 270 quiet trials are not filler. They tell us what happens with
  zero warning, which is the bottom of the curve. Without them it floats.

  Three. If warnings were frequent, people would slow down and wait for one,
  and then every measurement is of a cautious reach."

SLIDE 11 — SCOPE
================================================================================

ON SLIDE
  WE CAN SAY
     "Warn later than X ms and most users cannot stop,
      no matter how good the cue is."

  WE CANNOT SAY
     X is the best warning time
     What users do with an unreliable system
     What users do when not told to stop

SAY
  "We tell people to stop. So we measure what the motor system CAN do, not
  what a user WOULD do. A real user also has to notice and decide. That makes
  our number a lower bound — real systems need more, not less. I would rather
  say that up front than have a reviewer say it."

IF HE ASKS  (keep these as hidden backup slides)
================================================================================

Q: Why virtual obstacles?
   Safety — people need to nearly hit things at speed. Control — the hazard
   must move each trial so it cannot be memorised. And the thing we measure is
   a limit of the motor system, which does not care if the obstacle is real.

Q: Why not use reaction time from the literature?
   Reaction time is how fast you START something. This is how fast you STOP
   something already moving, while still wanting to reach the target.

Q: How do you know when they would have hit it?
   We freeze the hand's speed and direction at the buzz and project forward.
   Never updated afterwards, or someone who stopped successfully would look
   like they were warned infinitely early. We validate it against trials where
   they actually did hit.

Q: Isn't it obvious that more warning is better?
   The direction is obvious. The number is not, and the number is what you
   build with. "Warn earlier" is not implementable. "Warn at least 340 ms
   before contact" is.

Q: Why 24-30 participants?
   Simulation. The curve is fitted within each person from 90 warning trials,
   so trials per person matter more than people. Re-run with pilot data before
   freezing.

Q: What if the interval is too wide to recommend anything?
   We say so. That would mean the quantity is more variable than the field
   assumes, which is worth knowing.

Q: How does this relate to Paper 1?
   Independent. Different task, different people, different question. Paper 1
   compares two warning policies; this measures the deadline. Paper 1 needs
   nothing from this one, though it gets stronger once this number exists.
