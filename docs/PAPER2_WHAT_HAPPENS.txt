PAPER 2 — WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS IN THE STUDY
A concrete walkthrough. Written 2026-09-21.


================================================================================
PART A — WHAT IS PHYSICALLY IN THE ROOM
================================================================================

  The real room is EMPTY. Cleared. No furniture, no foam blocks, nothing to
  hit. About one metre of clear space in front of the participant is enough.
  This is not room-scale; they do not walk.

  The participant wears:
      - a VR headset
      - a vibrating tactor strapped to the right wrist
      - trackers (or holds a controller — decision still open)

  They stand, or sit. Their right hand rests at a "home" spot in front of
  their right hip, about waist-to-chest height.

  EVERYTHING ELSE IS VIRTUAL. There is nothing real to collide with.


================================================================================
PART B — THE SESSION, START TO FINISH
================================================================================

  0:00   Consent, screening, handedness. Safety briefing.
  0:15   Fit the headset. Place trackers. Strap on the wrist tactor.
  0:25   CUE CHECK. We buzz the tactor a few times and confirm they can feel
         it every time. The tactor then stays on for the whole session.
  0:30   READ THE INSTRUCTION SCRIPT OUT LOUD, word for word.
  0:35   PRACTICE — 16 trials. Not recorded. They learn the two speeds and
         the stop response.
  0:40   MAIN BLOCK — 360 trials. Break every 60 trials.
  1:35   Questionnaires: comfort, sickness, and "what do you think this
         study was testing?"
  1:45   Done.


================================================================================
PART C — WHAT THEY ARE TOLD TO DO
================================================================================

  The whole instruction is two rules:

      1. A ball appears. Reach out and touch it, at the speed the display
         asks for. Then come back to the start.

      2. If you feel a buzz on your wrist, STOP and pull your hand straight
         back. Do not finish the reach.

  That is it. They are NOT told to avoid anything. They are NOT told there
  are boxes to dodge. We tell them invisible zones exist and that the buzz
  means they are heading into one — but also that they cannot predict where
  the zones are and are not expected to.

  The buzz is the only information they ever get.


================================================================================
PART D — ONE ORDINARY TRIAL (3 out of every 4)
================================================================================

  t = 0.0 s    The word NORMAL appears, large, in front of them.
               (Or FAST. It tells them which speed to use.)

  t = 0.8 s    The word disappears. A ball appears about 70 cm away,
               somewhere in front of them — the direction changes every
               trial, up to 35 degrees left or right, 18 degrees up or down.

  t ~ 0.9 s    They start reaching.

  t ~ 1.4 s    THEIR HAND PASSES THROUGH AN INVISIBLE BOX.
               Nothing happens. No buzz, no sound, no flash. The box has no
               physical presence at all — it is a region in software.
               They do not know it happened. They never find out.

  t ~ 1.9 s    They touch the ball.

  t ~ 2.0 s    A short message: "good pace", or "too slow", or "too fast".
               This is about their SPEED only. It says nothing about the box.

  t ~ 2.5 s    "return to home". They bring the hand back.
               Next trial starts when they are back.

  WHAT WE RECORDED: their hand entered the box. That counts as a failure to
  avoid — with zero warning. This is the bottom of our curve.


================================================================================
PART E — ONE WARNING TRIAL (1 out of every 4)
================================================================================

  Identical to the above until the reach is underway.

  t = 0.0 s    NORMAL appears.
  t = 0.8 s    Ball appears.
  t ~ 0.9 s    They start reaching.

  ~~~~ now the software is watching every frame, 90 times a second ~~~~

               It tracks: how far the hand is from the invisible box, and
               how fast it is closing. It divides one by the other to get
               "time until contact".

  t ~ 1.15 s   Time-until-contact drops to the value assigned for this
               trial. THE CUE FIRES. The wrist buzzes.

  t ~ 1.20 s   They feel it. They stop pushing forward and pull back.

  t ~ 1.4 s    The hand stops — either short of the box (SUCCESS) or
               inside it (FAILURE).

  t ~ 2.5 s    "return to home". No feedback about whether they succeeded.
               Deliberately. If we told them, they would start guessing
               where the boxes are.

  WHAT WE RECORDED: how much warning they actually got, and whether they
  stopped in time. One dot on the curve.


================================================================================
PART F — THE ONE THING THAT MAKES IT A STUDY
================================================================================

  The buzz does not always come at the same moment. Each warning trial is
  assigned one of five timings:

      aimed at:      80    150    250    350    500  ms before contact
      actually got:  ~60   ~120   ~210   ~280   ~400 ms

  They are mixed randomly. The participant cannot tell which one they are
  getting until it happens.

  So across 90 warning trials, one person gets about 18 at each timing.
  Some are easy to stop. Some are impossible. Most are in between.

  THAT SPREAD IS THE EXPERIMENT. Plot "how much warning" against "did they
  stop", and you get the curve.


================================================================================
PART G — WHY THE TWO ROWS ABOVE ARE DIFFERENT
================================================================================

  We aim for 250 ms and 210 ms arrives. Three reasons:

      - tracking updates 90 times a second, so timing is chunky
      - the tactor takes tens of milliseconds to actually start buzzing
      - we are PREDICTING when contact would happen, and the hand is still
        speeding up, so the prediction runs slightly short

  We do not fight this. We aim roughly, then measure what actually landed,
  and plot the curve against the measured value.

  This is the single most important design decision in the paper.


================================================================================
PART H — WHAT THE PARTICIPANT KNOWS vs WHAT THE SOFTWARE KNOWS
================================================================================

  PARTICIPANT KNOWS                    SOFTWARE KNOWS
  --------------------------------     --------------------------------------
  Where the ball is                    Where the ball is
  Which speed was asked for            Which speed was asked for
  Whether they felt a buzz             Exactly when the buzz was commanded
  Whether they hit the ball            Exactly when it physically started
                                       Hand position, 90 times a second
  NOT where the boxes are              Where the box is
  NOT whether they entered one         Whether the hand entered it
  NOT how early the buzz came          How much warning was really delivered
  NOT their score                      Everything


================================================================================
PART I — HOW THE TRIALS ARE ARRANGED
================================================================================

  Trials come in groups of four. Exactly ONE trial in each group of four is
  a warning trial, in a random position within the group.

  Why: if warnings clustered together, the participant would notice a run and
  start bracing. One per four, always, means they can never predict it and
  can never relax.

  Group 1:   go    go    BUZZ  go
  Group 2:   BUZZ  go    go    go
  Group 3:   go    go    go    BUZZ
  ...90 groups, 360 trials.


================================================================================
PART J — WHAT COMES OUT AT THE END
================================================================================

  One row of data per trial. 360 rows per person. The two columns that matter:

      how much warning was actually delivered  (blank on ordinary trials)
      did the hand enter the box               (yes / no)

  Stack up 24-30 people and fit a curve:

      x-axis:  warning delivered, 0 to ~450 ms
      y-axis:  chance of stopping in time

      At 0 ms:     they almost always hit it   <- from the ordinary trials
      At ~400 ms:  they almost always stop it
      In between:  the curve rises

  Read off the point where it crosses 80%. That is LT80. That is the paper.
